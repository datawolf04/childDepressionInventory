# Annotated Bibliography: The Children's Depression Inventory (CDI)

**Source Document:** Kovacs, M., & MHS Staff. (2019). *Children's Depression Inventory 2nd Edition (CDI 2)* [Product overview]. American Psychological Association. https://www.apa.org/obesity-guideline/depression-inventory.pdf

This annotated bibliography surveys key academic literature on the Children's Depression Inventory (CDI) and its second edition (CDI 2), a widely used self-report measure of depressive symptoms in youth aged 7–17.

---

## Journal Articles

Saylor, C. F., Finch, A. J., Spirito, A., & Bennett, B. (1984). The Children's Depression Inventory: A systematic evaluation of psychometric properties. [*Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 52*(6), 955–967](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.52.6.955).

This landmark study provided the first systematic, independent evaluation of the CDI's psychometric properties across both community and clinical populations. The authors administered the CDI to 294 schoolchildren (ages 7–13) and 269 psychiatric inpatients (ages 7–16). Results demonstrated good internal consistency in both groups (α ≈ .80–.86), but test–retest reliability varied considerably across populations and retest intervals, raising questions about the instrument's stability over time. Validity analyses showed the CDI could distinguish children with general emotional distress from normal schoolchildren, yet differences between depressed (DSM-III diagnosed) and nondepressed psychiatric patients were not significant. The authors found that the CDI overlapped substantially with measures of anxiety, suggesting it captures a multidimensional construct broader than depression alone. This study established essential benchmarks for CDI interpretation and underscored the need for caution when using the instrument for diagnostic purposes.

---

Fristad, M. A., Emery, B. L., & Beck, S. J. (1997). Use and abuse of the children's depression inventory. [*Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 65*(4), 699–702](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.65.4.699).

This critical methodological review investigated how researchers were actually using the CDI in published studies of childhood depression. Reviewing recently published research, the authors found that half of all studies used the CDI, but 68% did not employ a clinical or structured interview to confirm diagnostic status. Most concerningly, 44% of studies that used the CDI alone referred to high-scoring children as "depressed" without providing cautionary statements about the instrument's limitations for diagnosis. The authors drew explicit parallels to earlier critiques of the Beck Depression Inventory, arguing that self-report inventories cannot substitute for clinical diagnosis. This paper served as an important corrective, prompting researchers to exercise greater rigor in interpreting CDI scores and to clearly distinguish between depressive symptomatology and clinical depression in their reporting.

Huang, C., & Dong, N. (2014). Dimensionality of the Children's Depression Inventory: Meta-analysis of pattern matrices. [*Journal of Child and Family Studies, 23*(7), 1182–1192](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-013-9779-1).

This meta-analysis systematically examined the factor structure of the CDI across 24 independent studies comprising 36 samples (N = 18,897). The authors found that a five-factor structure generally emerged across studies, but the content of these factors—labelled Self-Depreciation, Somatic Concerns, Externalizing, Lack of Personal and Social Interest, and Dysphoric Mood—differed from Kovacs's original five-factor conceptualization. Notably, the equivalence of this structure did not hold for at-risk or clinical samples, nor for non-English versions of the CDI. Clinical samples exhibited an independent Depressive Mood and Loneliness factor, while non-English administrations produced a distinct Sadness and Somatic factor. The authors recommended revising or removing certain self-depreciation items that cross-loaded poorly. This study provided the most comprehensive quantitative synthesis of CDI factor research and highlighted the influence of culture and clinical status on the instrument's dimensionality.

Twenge, J. M., & Nolen-Hoeksema, S. (2002). Age, gender, race, socioeconomic status, and birth cohort differences on the Children's Depression Inventory: A meta-analysis. [*Journal of Abnormal Psychology, 111*(4), 578–588](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.111.4.578).

This large-scale meta-analysis aggregated data from over 300 samples to examine demographic and cohort differences in CDI scores. The authors found that CDI scores followed a U-shaped age trajectory, decreasing from age 8 to 12 and then increasing from age 12 to 16, with girls showing a sharper rise during adolescence. Females scored consistently higher than males beginning around age 13. Race and socioeconomic status showed small but significant effects, with minority and lower-SES youth reporting slightly higher scores. Critically, the analysis revealed a secular trend: CDI scores increased substantially across birth cohorts from the 1980s through the 1990s, suggesting generational rises in depressive symptomatology. The authors recommended that new normative data be collected to account for these cohort effects. This study remains essential for interpreting CDI scores in context of demographic variables and historical change.

Jelínek, M., Květon, P., Burešová, I., & Klimusová, H. (2021). Measuring depression in adolescence: Evaluation of a hierarchical factor model of the Children's Depression Inventory and measurement invariance across boys and girls. [*PLoS ONE, 16*(4), e0249943](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0249943).

This recent study evaluated the factor structure of the CDI in a Czech adolescent sample (N = 2,206) using confirmatory factor analysis, addressing ongoing debates about the instrument's dimensionality. The authors found support for a hierarchical model with five first-order factors and one second-order factor, aligning with Kovacs's original conceptualization. Measurement invariance testing across gender revealed full configural and metric invariance, indicating that the CDI assesses the same constructs in boys and girls. However, scalar invariance was only partially achieved, suggesting some item-level biases. Girls showed higher latent means on the Negative Mood and Anhedonia factors. The study also showed good internal consistency (α = .86–.89) and provided Czech normative data. This paper contributes contemporary evidence supporting the hierarchical structure of the CDI and demonstrates its applicability across gender groups, while underscoring the need for culturally specific validation.

## Books/Manuals

Kovacs, M. (1992). *Children's Depression Inventory (CDI) manual*. [Multi-Health Systems.](https://storefront.mhs.com/collections/cdi-2)

Kovacs's original manual introduced the CDI as a 27-item self-report measure designed to assess the severity of depressive symptoms in children and adolescents. Modelled after the Beck Depression Inventory, the CDI presents each item as three graded statements representing absence of symptoms, mild symptoms, or definite symptoms. Kovacs proposed a hierarchical factor structure comprising five first-order factors (Negative Mood, Interpersonal Problems, Ineffectiveness, Anhedonia, and Negative Self-Esteem) and a single second-order factor of general depression. The manual reported adequate internal consistency (α ≈ .80–.89) and test–retest reliability across community and clinical samples. This publication established the CDI as the most widely used instrument for childhood depression screening and remains the foundational reference for all subsequent research on the measure.

---

Kovacs, M., & MHS Staff. (2011). *Children's Depression Inventory 2nd edition (CDI 2) technical manual*. [Multi-Health Systems.](https://storefront.mhs.com/products/cdi-2-manual)

The CDI 2 technical manual documents a comprehensive revision of the original CDI, incorporating advances in childhood depression research. Key refinements include an expanded 28-item self-report form, revised scales, and updated normative data drawn from 1,100 children aged 7–17 across 26 U.S. states. The CDI 2 introduced a multi-rater assessment model with parallel parent (17-item) and teacher (12-item) forms alongside the self-report, along with a 12-item short form for efficient screening. The manual reports strong psychometric properties, including internal consistency (α = .80–.92 across forms), test–retest reliability, and convergent/discriminant validity. The CDI 2 also includes Spanish-language translations. This revision modernized the instrument while preserving continuity with the original, maintaining its status as the leading self-report measure for pediatric depression.

Kovacs, M. (2015). Children's Depression Inventory (CDI and CDI 2). [*The Encyclopedia of Clinical Psychology*, 1–5. Portico.](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118625392.wbecp419)

This encyclopedia entry provides a synoptic overview of both the original CDI and the CDI 2, authored by the instrument's developer. Kovacs describes the development, administration, scoring, and psychometric properties of both versions, including the multi-rater assessment model introduced with the CDI 2. The entry covers the hierarchical factor structure, normative data, and clinical utility of the instruments, and summarises key validity evidence. As a consolidated reference from the original author, this work serves as an accessible entry point for clinicians and researchers seeking a comprehensive yet concise summary of both the CDI and CDI 2.
